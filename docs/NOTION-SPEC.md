## Project brief
- Browser-only medieval settlement and castle-building game, inspired by Stronghold, without combat.
- Implementation constraint: TypeScript exclusively in the frontend; no backend services or server-side game logic.
- Ask and confirm one multiple-choice design question at a time; always provide Other / custom answer. Build after sufficient requirements are gathered.
## Confirmed decisions
1. **Core gameplay:** Combined medieval simulation — Stronghold-like castle building, Anno-like production chains and Banished-like population management; peaceful gameplay, no battles.
2. **Graphics and camera:** Classic Stronghold-style isometric 2D.
3. **World and map generation:** Fully procedural terrain and resource generation for every new game; reproducible, shareable map seeds. No handcrafted map dependency in the core design.
4. **Building and construction:** Classic Stronghold tile-grid construction: fixed-tile building placement, roads, walls, towers and farmland; grid-aware placement validation and pathfinding.
5. **Economy and logistics:** Classic Stronghold-style economy with simple production chains and physical goods transport. Workers produce goods in workshops and carry resources between production buildings and storage. This decision sets economy depth to simple, despite the broader Anno inspiration in decision 1.
6. **Population and citizen management:** Classic Stronghold-style population: immigration rate depends on popularity; available population supplies workers; food, taxes, housing and basic happiness drive popularity. No family, aging or deep individual-citizen life-cycle simulation required.
7. **Seasons and environment:** Four seasonal states (spring, summer, autumn, winter) with visual changes, seasonal farming and harvest timing, food reserves for winter, and moderate environmental challenges. Keep environmental mechanics accessible rather than a highly detailed climate simulator.
8. **Objectives and progression:** Settlement progression: start with a small village and unlock more advanced buildings and production chains as the population and settlement prosperity grow. The progression model must gate construction availability; exact unlock thresholds and stages are to be designed later.
9. **Castle role:** Functional castles as medieval administrative and economic centers rather than principally decorative or elaborate defensive structures. Keep, treasury, courthouse and town hall-type functions can unlock governance, taxation and population/economic benefits. No combat.
10. **Trade and marketplace:** Classic Stronghold-style marketplace with instantaneous buying and selling of resources at fixed prices. No merchant-cart simulation, variable market pricing, or regional trade routes needed.
11. **World scale and expansion:** Expanding, chunk-based procedural world with unlockable terrain. The map can grow beyond the starting region; use deterministic seed-derived chunk generation and chunk-aware rendering/simulation, without committing to an unbounded world size or an inhabitant-count performance guarantee. Territory unlocking uses purchased adjacent chunks with increasing gold prices (decision 12).
12. **Territory expansion:** Purchase adjacent territory chunks using treasury gold; territory costs increase as the settlement acquires more land. No exploration units, fog-of-war prerequisite, or automatic population-based unlocks required.
13. **Simulation pacing:** Classic Stronghold-style real-time simulation with pause and 1×, 2× and 4× speed controls. Use a deterministic fixed simulation timestep decoupled from visual rendering, so production, economy and seasonal cycles respond predictably at different speeds.
14. **Building construction:** Instant construction in the classic Stronghold style. When sufficient required resources are available, deduct them immediately and place the completed building instantly. No construction timers, stages, building crews or material-delivery tasks are required. This does not change physical goods transport for ongoing production (decision 5).
15. **Roads and worker movement:** Roads are optional. Workers can navigate any traversable terrain and select routes that favor roads due to faster movement; off-road movement is permitted. Use weighted tile-grid pathfinding, accounting for traversability and movement speed, with physical goods transport as established in decision 5.
16. **Resource storage:** Classic Stronghold-style stockpiles hold construction and production materials, and granaries hold food. Workers physically deliver produced resources to storage. Each storage building has limited capacity, which players expand by building additional stockpiles or granaries. Keep the storage interface straightforward rather than implementing complex warehouse routing.
17. **Production catalog and extensibility:** Progressive economy launching with approximately 15–20 resource types and basic medieval production chains. Resource types, building recipes, inputs/outputs and related unlock rules should be defined via data-driven TypeScript configuration to allow later additional industries without rewriting the simulation engine. Keep initial scope small and production mechanics Stronghold-simple.
18. **Worker assignment:** Classic Stronghold-style automatic staffing. Each operating production building claims one available worker automatically. Unstaffed buildings remain inactive until workers become available; no manual assignment, labor-priority system, or shifts required.
19. **Popularity, food rations, taxes and services:** Classic Stronghold-style popularity system with player-adjustable food rations, tax rates and food variety. Ale, religion and entertainment provide further popularity bonuses; popularity drives immigration or emigration. These factors also integrate with food stocks, treasury and unlocked civic/economic buildings.
20. **Savegames and persistence:** Entirely frontend-local persistence using IndexedDB for automatic saves and multiple user-controlled manual save slots. Support portable savegame export to a file and import from a file, using explicitly versioned save formats and migration logic for compatibility across game updates. No backend storage or account dependency.
21. **Rendering framework:** Phaser as the frontend TypeScript 2D game framework for sprite rendering, camera, input handling, animation and scenes. Implement classic isometric presentation on top of Phaser, with game rules and economic simulation maintained separately from rendering for testability.
22. **Art direction and assets:** Original, detailed, illustrated 2D isometric sprites and character animations inspired by the visual richness of classic Stronghold. Create original terrain, buildings, vegetation and animated inhabitants rather than relying on copied Stronghold assets or choosing a pixel-art/procedural-placeholder-first visual style. Organize assets in reusable sprite sheets/atlases compatible with Phaser.
23. **Game interface and controls:** Modernized Stronghold-style interface: bottom construction categories, compact top resource indicators, minimap, contextual building and management panels, and responsive controls/layout. Integrate UI with Phaser while keeping simulation logic independent.
24. **Supported devices and input modes:** Desktop-first design with mouse and keyboard as primary controls; tablets are supported through touch interactions including pinch-to-zoom, drag-to-pan and adapted construction toolbars. Smartphones are not a primary release target. Keep UI responsive without compromising desktop gameplay.
25. **Settlement scale and performance:** Target massive medieval cities with approximately 5,000–10,000 inhabitants and thousands of buildings. Plan aggressive optimization, chunk-based rendering, efficient pathfinding, selective/level-of-detail simulation, and Web Workers for off-main-thread computation. Treat these as performance targets requiring benchmark validation, not guaranteed capacity or fixed population caps.
26. **Frontend UI framework:** React and CSS for responsive construction controls, top resource indicators, minimap-adjacent interface, contextual panels, statistics and savegame screens; Phaser renders the isometric 2D world. Both communicate with an independently implemented, frontend-only TypeScript simulation. Keep game simulation, rendering and interface state cleanly separated.
27. **Parallel simulation architecture:** Use multiple specialized browser Web Workers for economy, population, environment and pathfinding to support the massive-city performance target. Phaser and React remain responsible for rendering and UI on the main thread. Cross-worker coordination uses an authoritative coordinator and deterministic tick-boundary updates (decision 28).
28. **Cross-worker state synchronization:** One authoritative coordinator owns canonical world state and advances deterministic fixed simulation ticks. Specialized economy, population, environment and pathfinding workers receive controlled snapshots, calculate results independently, and return batched messages. The coordinator validates/applies results only at defined tick boundaries, avoiding worker-side competing writes and nondeterministic state mutation.
29. **Offline play and deployment:** Distribute as an installable, offline-capable Progressive Web App (PWA) served from static hosting. Use a service worker to cache the application shell and game assets for offline play after initial installation/loading, while IndexedDB retains local saves and manual save slots; preserve import/export portability. No gameplay backend, hosted database or user account required.
30. **First release scope and delivery strategy:** Full-featured initial release implementing all confirmed game design and architectural decisions before public release, not a reduced playable vertical slice as the released product. Internal prototypes, incremental engineering milestones and automated testing are acceptable as implementation tactics but do not narrow the release commitment.
31. **Initial resource catalog:** Balanced medieval economy with exactly 18 physical resources: wood, stone, iron ore, iron, tools, coal, wheat, flour, bread, apples, fish, meat, milk, cheese, hops, ale, wool and cloth. Gold is a separate treasury currency, not a physical stockpile resource. Further production buildings, recipe rates and progression details remain to be specified.
32. **Production-building specialization:** Classic Stronghold-style specialization: each production building type has one primary production function/recipe. Examples include wheat farm → wheat, mill → flour, bakery → bread, and brewery → ale. No selectable multi-recipe workshop model or required upgrade/variant system. Final building catalog and recipes remain to be defined.
33. **Initial building catalog scope:** Focused Stronghold-style catalog of approximately 30–35 building types for the complete first public release. Include essential specialized production buildings for the confirmed 18 physical resources, housing, stockpiles/granaries, marketplace, governance and taxation, religion/entertainment civic services, and basic castle walls, towers and gates. Favor functional coverage over decorative variety. Exact catalog finalized in decision 34.
34. **Exact initial building catalog (31 types):** 18 specialized production buildings: woodcutter, quarry, iron mine, smelter, toolsmith, coal mine, wheat farm, mill, bakery, apple orchard, fishing hut, hunting lodge, dairy farm, cheesemaker, hop farm, brewery, sheep farm and weaver. 13 non-production/building-tool types: stockpile, granary, marketplace, hovel, keep, treasury, tavern, chapel, fairground, wall, gatehouse, tower and road. Exclude the larger house and courthouse from the initial release. Keep the catalog fixed at these 31 types unless changed by a later explicit decision.
35. **Batched physical production logistics:** An automatically assigned worker can carry multiple production cycles' worth of ingredients or finished goods per delivery trip, using small local input/output buffers at each production building. Actual worker movement and storage capacity continue to constrain logistics; batching minimizes unnecessary trips and pathfinding costs at large-city scale. Exact carrying quantities and buffer capacities remain subject to balancing.
36. **Game calendar and seasonal pacing:** At normal (1×) speed, a complete game year lasts 60 minutes of active simulation time, divided equally into four 15-minute seasons (spring, summer, autumn and winter). Pause freezes the game calendar; 2× and 4× speeds advance simulation time proportionally. Agriculture and food-reserve balancing must use this calendar.
37. **Agricultural growth and harvest cycles:** Seasonal planting and harvesting rather than continuous crop output. Spring is the planting period, summer is the growth period, autumn is the harvest period, and winter is dormant for crop farming. Wheat, apple orchards and hops have crop-specific growth/harvest behavior. Crops maintain growth state over time; food reserves and stored autumn harvests support winter. Exact yields, inputs, harvest timing and crop-specific exceptions remain to be balanced.
38. **Winter non-crop food production:** Moderate seasonal effects: hunting continues through winter at its normal baseline unless later balance rules specify otherwise; fishing and dairy production slow in winter but do not stop; cheese production continues provided sufficient milk is available in production buffers or storage. Crop agriculture remains dormant during winter (decision 37). Seasonal output modifiers will be data-driven; exact values remain to be balanced.
39. **Population food distribution and variety:** Classic Stronghold-style settlement-wide shared food pool: inhabitants consume food from aggregated granary inventories, distributed evenly across the available food types; variety contributes a positive popularity modifier. Do not implement physical food deliveries to individual households or local-household food availability checks. Overall ration controls are fixed in decision 40. Per-food enable/disable controls are not required.
40. **Food ration levels and popularity:** Four discrete Stronghold-style ration levels: no food (0% of normal consumption), half rations (50%), normal rations (100%) and double rations (200%). Low/zero rations reduce popularity, normal rations are neutral baseline, and double rations increase popularity. Consumption scales with the chosen ration level and is allocated evenly among available food types per decision 39. Exact numeric popularity modifiers and baseline per-capita food consumption remain to be balanced.
41. **Taxation and subsidies:** Classic Stronghold-style nine-level taxation scheme consisting of four increasing subsidy/financial-support levels, one zero-tax level, and four increasing tax levels. Subsidies debit treasury gold and increase popularity; taxes generate gold and reduce popularity proportionally to the chosen level. Tax/subsidy effects are based on settlement population and integrated with the existing popularity and immigration system. Exact rates and popularity modifiers to be balanced.
42. **Popularity-driven immigration and emigration:** Classic Stronghold-style popularity scale from 0 to 100, with a neutral threshold of 50. When popularity is greater than 50, immigrants arrive provided that housing capacity is available; when popularity is less than 50, residents emigrate; at exactly 50, population remains stable. Immigration/emigration speed scales with the distance from 50. Exact migration-rate curves, arrival intervals and starting population remain to be specified; housing capacity is fixed in decision 43.
43. **Hovel housing capacity and aggregation:** Each hovel provides housing for exactly 8 inhabitants. Housing capacity is shared across the entire settlement and equals 8 multiplied by the number of operational hovels. Population and vacant housing are calculated globally; no resident-to-household assignments or individual household tracking. Immigration requires available settlement-wide housing capacity (decision 42).
44. **Starting settlement conditions:** New games begin with a pre-built keep, stockpile, granary, and two hovels, with 8 initial inhabitants and enough construction materials, food, and treasury gold to establish the first production chains. The two hovels provide shared housing capacity for 16, leaving space for 8 immigrants. Exact starting supplies are defined by decision 45; starting building placement remains to be specified.
45. **Exact starting inventory and treasury:** Every new game starts with 100 wood, 50 stone, 60 bread, 40 apples and 500 gold. All other physical resources start at zero. Gold is treasury currency and is not stored in the stockpile. This is in addition to the pre-built keep, stockpile, granary, two hovels and eight inhabitants defined by decision 44. Validate early building costs and food consumption against this opening balance.
46. **Terrain and purchasable territory chunk dimensions:** Each chunk is exactly 64 × 64 fixed isometric world tiles (4,096 tiles total). The same chunk grid is used for deterministic terrain generation, chunk-aware rendering/simulation, and gold-purchased adjacent territory unlocks. Terrain content is derived from the shareable world seed and chunk coordinates. Precise map-generation features and purchase price progression remain to be specified.
47. **Territory purchase price drivers:** Gold purchase costs for adjacent 64 × 64-tile territory chunks increase with both the settlement's current population and the total number of previously purchased chunks. Price must account for both factors, not just a fixed linear per-purchase increment. Exact formula finalized in decision 48.
48. **Territory purchase price formula:** Cost in treasury gold for the next adjacent 64 × 64-tile chunk = round((100 + 50 × previously purchased chunk count) × (1 + current settlement population / 1000)). Use integer population and the number of successfully purchased expansion chunks (excluding the initial owned starting chunk); round to the nearest whole gold at purchase-time. Eligibility remains limited to chunks sharing an edge with owned territory. Prices scale multiplicatively with both territorial expansion and population.
49. **Procedural terrain features and elevation:** Classic mostly flat Stronghold-style isometric terrain. Generate grasslands, forests, lakes, rivers, and natural resource deposits deterministically from the world seed across 64 × 64-tile chunks. Do not implement meaningful elevation, slopes, cliffs, or height-dependent building/pathfinding rules. Terrain and water passability, placement restrictions, seamless cross-chunk river connections, and biome/resource distribution remain to be specified. Starting-region resource guarantees are defined in decision 50.
50. **Starting-region resource guarantees:** The initial owned 64 × 64-tile chunk must contain sufficient buildable grassland, forest, farmland and stone for the early settlement. The group of four directly edge-adjacent purchasable chunks (north, south, east and west) must collectively guarantee access to fishable water, iron ore, coal and additional resource deposits; these are not individually required in every neighboring chunk. Guarantee placement is seed-deterministic and should preserve cross-chunk terrain coherence, ensuring all seeds permit early progression while giving meaningful incentives to expand.
51. **Finite, regenerating natural-resource reserves:** Every resource deposit or natural harvesting area has a finite local stock that decreases with extraction or harvesting and gradually replenishes over simulation time. This includes stone, iron ore and coal deposits as well as forests, fish populations and wildlife. Mineral deposits do not remain permanently exhausted and resource access is never intrinsically one-use; extraction must respect local availability. Regeneration rates and depleted-node recovery are fixed in decision 52; individual maximum reserves and extraction quantities remain to be balanced.
52. **Natural resource regeneration rates and automatic restart:** Each mineral node (stone, iron ore, coal) regenerates 10% of its local maximum reserve per 60-minute in-game year. Forests regenerate 50% of local maximum reserves per game year; fish and wildlife regenerate 100% per game year. Regeneration occurs over simulation time, never increases stock beyond the node's maximum, and remains compatible with pause/1×/2×/4×. Resource extraction automatically pauses when insufficient local stock exists for the next harvesting/production operation and resumes when enough reserve is replenished. Exact node capacities and per-operation extraction amounts remain configurable for balancing.
53. **Uniform production-cycle cadence:** Every non-seasonal production building completes one production cycle every 30 seconds of active simulation time at 1× speed (60 seconds/minute, adjusted by pause/2×/4×). Building recipes may differ in ingredients and output quantity, but not in the baseline cycle duration. Production stalls when workers, inputs, local resource reserves or buffer capacity are unavailable, and resumes when restored; batched physical collection and delivery remain as decided in 35. Seasonal crop buildings retain their spring planting, summer growth, autumn harvest and winter dormancy mechanics from decision 37 rather than producing uniformly every 30 seconds. Winter reductions for fishing and dairy remain applicable under decision 38. Recipe quantities are defined by decision 54; seasonal output adjustments remain to be specified.
54. **Simple Stronghold-style production recipes:** Each non-seasonal production cycle normally creates one unit of its designated physical output resource. A processing building consumes one unit of each ingredient required by its recipe per completed cycle, unless an explicit future exception is agreed. Examples: mill consumes 1 wheat to produce 1 flour; bakery consumes 1 flour to produce 1 bread; smelter consumes 1 iron ore and 1 coal to produce 1 iron. Extraction buildings output 1 resource unit per cycle subject to local availability. Seasonal crop production retains its distinct growth-and-harvest rules, rather than forcing per-cycle crop output. Recipes remain data-driven TypeScript definitions; logistics batches may contain multiple completed cycles of goods without modifying conversion ratios. Exact processing recipes and ingredient quantities are fixed in decision 90.
55. **Construction-cost categories:** Production buildings and housing are constructed instantly using physical construction materials only; they require no treasury gold. Civic/service, administrative and castle buildings or structures require construction materials plus a gold payment from the treasury. Apply this distinction to the 31 confirmed building types; exact per-building wood, stone, iron, tools and gold requirements remain to be specified and balanced. Ordinary roads are treated as infrastructure with materials-only costs unless a future explicit decision changes this.
56. **Hybrid Stronghold-style construction-cost system:** Use standardized, low construction-material costs for basic production buildings and hovels; define individually balanced construction-material requirements for advanced industry and individually balanced material-plus-gold requirements for civic, administrative and castle buildings or structures. Store all costs explicitly in data-driven TypeScript building definitions, not hard-coded simulation logic. Preserve instant construction (decision 14) and the gold/material category distinction (decision 55). Exact numerical prices remain subject to balancing against the confirmed starting inventory in decision 45.
57. **Permanent settlement progression stages:** The player advances through four stages: Hamlet at population 0+, Village at 50+, Town at 200+, and City at 1,000+. Beyond Hamlet, advancement additionally requires positive popularity and adequate food reserves. Once a stage is reached, its buildings and recipes stay permanently unlocked even if population, popularity or food reserves subsequently decline. Exact advancement prerequisites are defined in decision 58; the assignment of the 31 building types to stages remains to be specified.
58. **Food reserves and popularity required for stage advancement:** To unlock Village (50+ inhabitants), Town (200+) or City (1,000+), the settlement must simultaneously have population at or above the stage threshold, popularity strictly above 50 on the 0–100 scale, and enough food stored in the shared granaries to feed its current population for 10 minutes of active simulation time at the normal (100%) ration consumption rate. Evaluate this requirement using normal ration demand regardless of the player's currently selected ration level. The check does not consume food as a progression fee. Once a stage is achieved its unlocks are permanent, even if reserves, population or popularity later fall. Exact per-capita normal food consumption remains to be balanced.
59. **Exact building unlock stages (31 types total):** Assign all 31 building types permanently to four stages, with stages unlocked using the population, popularity and food-reserve requirements established in decisions 57–58. Hamlet (13 types): keep, stockpile, granary, hovel, road, woodcutter, quarry, wheat farm, mill, bakery, apple orchard, hunting lodge, marketplace. Village (7 additional types): fishing hut, dairy farm, cheesemaker, sheep farm, weaver, chapel, treasury. Town (8 additional types): iron mine, coal mine, smelter, toolsmith, hop farm, brewery, tavern, wall. City (3 additional types): fairground, gatehouse, tower. Earlier-stage buildings remain available after advancing; the existing pre-built starting buildings are present from the beginning. Store these stage assignments in the TypeScript building registry.
60. **Normal food consumption baseline:** At normal (100%) rations and 1× speed, the settlement consumes exactly 1 food unit per 10 inhabitants per active simulation minute, i.e. food units per minute = population / 10. The four ration levels in decision 40 multiply this baseline by 0, 0.5, 1 or 2. Consume from the shared granary food pool under decision 39; track fractional demand deterministically without systematic loss from integer rounding. For the 10-minute normal-ration reserves required to advance settlement stage (decision 58), stored food units must be at least equal to current population (e.g. 50 inhabitants require 50 units). The rate scales with simulation speed and pauses with the game.
61. **Strong food-ration popularity modifiers:** The four ration levels have fixed additive modifiers to the settlement's 0–100 popularity score: no food (0% ration) −50; half rations (50%) −20; normal rations (100%) 0; double rations (200%) +20. The modifier reflects the current selected ration setting and is recalculated from current game state, not accumulated each tick. Actual food consumption remains governed by decisions 39, 40 and 60; popularity is clamped to the 0–100 scale after combining applicable factors. Further rules for stock shortages and other factors are to be defined during balancing.
62. **Balanced nine-level taxation and subsidies:** The tax/subsidy setting is an integer from −4 through +4, where negative levels are subsidies, 0 is neutral, and positive levels are taxes. Each positive tax level generates 2 gold per 100 current inhabitants per active simulation minute and imposes a fixed −5 popularity modifier; each subsidy level costs 2 gold per 100 inhabitants per active minute and grants +5 popularity. Thus at tax level t, the treasury flow per minute is t × 2 × population / 100 gold and the popularity modifier is −5 × t; both are zero at level 0. At level +4, tax income is 8 gold per 100 inhabitants per minute and popularity is −20; at level −4, subsidy spending is 8 gold per 100 inhabitants per minute and popularity is +20. Apply exact simulation-time accumulation across pause and speed settings, avoiding rounding loss; popularity is recalculated from current state and clamped to 0–100. If treasury funds are insufficient for the next subsidy payment, the tax/subsidy setting automatically reverts to neutral (0), as fixed in decision 64.
63. **Consumption-based food-diversity popularity bonus:** Track the distinct edible food types actually consumed from the shared granaries in the rolling preceding 5 minutes of active simulation time. The six eligible types are bread, apples, fish, meat, milk and cheese; ale is not food and instead belongs to the tavern system. If N distinct types were consumed in this window, add 4 × max(0, N − 1) popularity points, capped at +20 for all six types. Zero or one actually consumed type yields zero bonus; stored stock alone does not count. Recalculate the bonus as the rolling window changes, with deterministic timestamps/ticks that respect pause and game-speed multipliers. Actual equalized food distribution remains governed by decision 39 and consumption volumes by decision 60. Exact minimum consumed quantity threshold for recognizing a type, if any, is left for implementation balancing.
64. **Automatic subsidy cancellation on insufficient treasury funds:** If available treasury gold cannot fully cover a due subsidy payment at the currently selected negative tax/subsidy level, automatically reset the setting to neutral (0). Stop subsidy spending and remove its associated popularity bonus immediately, without allowing negative treasury gold or partial subsidy payments. The player must explicitly choose a negative level again to re-enable subsidies after replenishing gold; subsidies do not automatically restart. Apply this deterministically at the payment tick under the gold-rate calculation in decision 62; gold accumulated from other sources remains available normally.
65. **Generous shared storage capacities:** Each granary provides capacity for 1,000 total food units, pooled across the six edible food resource types; each stockpile provides capacity for 500 total material/non-food resource units, pooled across all stored non-food physical resource types. Capacities add linearly for additional buildings: N granaries offer N × 1,000 food units and M stockpiles offer M × 500 non-food units. Shared capacity is not separately reserved per resource. Food and materials retain separate limits. Full storage prevents further inbound deliveries until space is freed; delivery routing and building-local buffers remain to be specified. Gold remains treasury currency and is never stored in stockpiles.
66. **Uniform worker transport capacity and partial loads:** Each physical worker transport trip may carry up to 10 total resource units, with the same unit capacity for all resource types and no resource-specific weight multipliers. Mixed-resource loads are prohibited by decision 77: every trip carries only one resource type. Workers may carry fewer than 10 units when fewer are available or needed. Preserve the existing one-worker-per-production-building and batched physical logistics decisions (18 and 35); carrying capacity limits transported quantities per trip, not the amount produced in a cycle. Exact local input/output buffers, dispatch triggers and routing remain to be specified.
67. **Large uniform production-building buffers:** Each production building has an input buffer with capacity for 50 total resource units and an independent output buffer with capacity for 50 total resource units. Each buffer's capacity is shared across all resource types it holds, not separately allocated for each ingredient or output. These are local building capacities in addition to the separate granary and stockpile capacity limits from decision 65. Production pauses when its output buffer lacks sufficient capacity for the next cycle or its required inputs are unavailable, and resumes after the condition clears. The maximum 10 units per physical worker trip established in decision 66 still applies; larger buffers allow multiple trips of goods to accumulate. Dispatch triggers are defined in decision 68; destination routing remains to be specified.
68. **Balanced batch transport dispatch and emergency priority:** Under normal operation, queue a finished-goods collection once at least 10 units of an output are available, and trigger input replenishment for each required ingredient when its local stock falls below 10 units. Preserve the 10-total-unit maximum per worker delivery trip (decision 66) and the independent 50-unit input/output building buffers (decision 67). Give emergency priority to deliveries that can restart a building stopped by missing recipe ingredients or blocked by a full output buffer; such emergency trips may use partial loads below 10 units. Prevent duplicate pending jobs and respect available storage and buffer capacity when dispatching. Destination routing is decided in decision 69; ties between competing emergency requests remain to be specified.
69. **Direct producer-to-consumer transport with storage fallback:** When goods are ready for transport, prefer direct deliveries from their production source to a nearby production building that currently needs the resource and has enough free input-buffer capacity. If there is no suitable nearby downstream demand, transport finished goods to the nearest eligible granary (food) or stockpile (other physical goods) with available capacity. Production buildings may also receive inputs collected from stored inventory. Workers follow the shortest practical traversable route by travel time, accounting for optional road speed bonuses rather than merely tile count; off-road movement remains possible (decision 15). Retain physical worker trips with up to 10 units, 50-unit building buffers and emergency delivery priority (decisions 66–68). Destination selection must avoid duplicate assignments or over-reservation of goods and capacity. The nearby eligibility threshold is fixed at 30 off-road-equivalent path tiles by decision 71 and road speed at 2× by decision 70. Base movement speed is fixed by decision 72; deterministic tie-breaking and job assignment remain implementation specifications.
70. **Roads double worker movement speed:** A worker moves at 2× its ordinary off-road speed while traversing road tiles. Workers may still move over any ordinarily traversable off-road tile, so construction of roads is optional rather than a strict connectivity requirement. Worker pathfinding must select traversable routes by estimated travel time, not simply tile count, charging half the corresponding off-road traversal time for a road tile. This applies to physical resource-delivery trips, including producer-to-consumer and storage routes (decision 69), and scales consistently with pause/1×/2×/4× simulation speed. Base movement is 2 off-road tiles or 4 road tiles per active simulation second at 1× (decision 72); deterministic route tie-breaking remains to be specified, and the nearby delivery range is fixed in decision 71.
71. **30 off-road-equivalent-tile direct-delivery limit:** Direct producer-to-consumer delivery is eligible only when the shortest traversable route by estimated travel time costs no more than 30 off-road-equivalent tiles. Each ordinary traversable off-road tile counts as 1 equivalent tile, and each road tile as 0.5 equivalent tile, consistent with the 2× road movement speed in decision 70. The threshold is evaluated along the route, not using straight-line distance. If no demanding production building with sufficient free input buffer is eligible within this route-cost limit, finished goods go to the nearest eligible granary or stockpile with available space under decision 69. Inputs can still come from storage. Evaluate paths deterministically, preserving capacity reservations and emergency priorities. Base movement speed is fixed in decision 72; deterministic route tie-breaking remains to be decided.
72. **Uniform worker base speed: 2 off-road or 4 road tiles per simulation second:** Every worker travels at 2 ordinarily traversable off-road tiles per second of active simulation time at 1×, and 4 road tiles per second due to the 2× road speed multiplier from decision 70. Use the same base speed for all worker roles, with no role-specific movement multiplier. Pathfinding continues to minimize travel time: 1 off-road tile costs 0.5 simulation seconds, while 1 road tile costs 0.25 simulation seconds at 1×. Thus a 30-off-road-equivalent-tile direct-delivery journey from decision 71 takes 15 active simulation seconds one way, excluding pickup/drop-off overhead if any. Movement pauses when the game is paused and runs 2×/4× faster in wall-clock time when simulation speed increases. Deterministic routing tie-breaks remain open; delivery jobs are restricted to each worker's own production building per decision 73.
73. **Dedicated transport workers per production building:** Each production building retains its one assigned worker (decision 18). That worker exclusively transports goods needed by their own production building and finished goods produced by that building. They must not accept logistics assignments for unrelated buildings, even when idle or when another building has an emergency. Their own output deliveries may go directly to eligible downstream production buildings or to storage; their own inputs may be picked up from producers or storage, following destination/routing decisions 68–72. Emergency priority applies when selecting among the worker's own eligible delivery jobs. Preserve the 10-unit load limit and physical movement. Production pauses whenever its assigned worker is away and resumes from suspended progress on return, as decided in 74.
74. **Production pauses during the dedicated worker's transport trips:** Each production building's sole assigned worker is required on site for production. When that worker departs to transport its building's inputs or outputs, the ongoing production cycle pauses immediately, retaining accrued progress rather than resetting it. Once the worker returns to the building, production resumes from that stored progress if required inputs, resource-node stock and output buffer capacity are available; otherwise it remains stalled until the corresponding blocker clears. The 30-second active production duration per completed non-seasonal cycle from decision 53 counts only time actually spent producing with the worker present, and scales with simulation speed. Workers still carry at most 10 units per trip (decision 66) and handle only their own building's logistics (decision 73). Priority between competing input and output trips is fixed in decision 75.
75. **Output-first transport priority with missing-input exception:** When a production building's dedicated worker has eligible output-collection and input-replenishment jobs pending simultaneously, dispatch the output collection first, provided the building is not currently blocked by missing required production inputs. If a missing ingredient is already preventing production, give that ingredient's replenishment job priority over output collection until production can restart. Continue honoring transport prerequisites and capacity reservations from decisions 66–69; the worker never services unrelated buildings (decision 73), and production pauses during the trip (decision 74). Once urgent missing-input work is resolved, return to output-first ordering. Deterministic tie-breaking among multiple eligible output destinations or missing ingredients remains a technical detail. Output below the regular 10-unit pickup threshold may be collected after 60 active simulation seconds without production, as decided in 76.
76. **Inactivity-triggered partial output collection after 60 simulation seconds:** The normal output-collection dispatch threshold remains 10 units (decision 68). If a production building has completed no new production cycle for 60 consecutive seconds of active simulation time, its assigned worker becomes eligible to transport any remaining output from that building even if fewer than 10 units are available, using a partial load up to the usual 10-unit trip limit (decision 66). No idle time elapses while the entire game is paused; game speed multipliers apply to simulation time. Dispatch remains subject to the worker being available, a valid destination and sufficient destination capacity, and the output-first/missing-input precedence fixed in decision 75. The partial-load rule covers mineral depletion, winter or other seasonal shutdowns, or other production interruptions without forcing inefficient tiny deliveries during normal operation. After another production cycle completes, restart the inactivity timer. A transport trip can carry only one resource type, as fixed in decision 77.
77. **Single-resource worker transport trips:** Each physical worker transport trip carries only one resource type, up to the universal 10-unit maximum in decision 66; partial loads remain permitted. Production buildings requiring several ingredient types must have their assigned worker make a separate trip for each ingredient type. Do not combine iron ore and coal, for example, in a single trip, even when both are available at the same source location. Output deliveries are also limited to a single resource type per trip. Keep the dedicated-worker model (decision 73), direct delivery with storage fallback (decision 69), output-first exception for missing inputs (decision 75), and 60-second inactivity-based partial output pickup (decision 76). When multiple inputs are low, select the ingredient supporting the fewest complete remaining production cycles, breaking ties by estimated delivery time and stable resource ID, per decision 78.
78. **Most-depleted ingredient replenishment priority:** If a production building has multiple required ingredient types needing replenishment, its dedicated worker prioritizes the ingredient with the fewest remaining complete production cycles, calculated as floor(available local input amount / units consumed per cycle) for each ingredient. This generalizes to different recipe input quantities even though the baseline is 1 unit per ingredient (decision 54). Among equally depleted inputs, choose the ingredient with the shortest estimated pickup-and-return delivery time; if still tied, sort by stable resource ID to preserve deterministic simulation. Missing-input emergencies retain precedence over output collection when production is already blocked (decision 75). Trips transport only one resource type up to 10 units (decision 77), and the ordinary restocking threshold remains below 10 local units (decision 68). This priority changes job selection only, not how much capacity is available or how many ingredients the recipe requires.

At 1× a building could theoretically complete up to 30 uninterrupted cycles over autumn, yielding up to 300 wheat, 240 apples, or 180 hops per harvest season, before pauses caused by its worker's deliveries, full output buffers, or any other blockers. Values are fixed as initial balance parameters in TypeScript data, rather than hard-coded in simulation logic.
